## Loglines

A logline is the whole story in one sentence: **who, what they want, what stands in the way, and the twist.** Save the Cat lists protagonist, goal and the obstacle; StudioBinder and Blake Snyder add irony. Aim for one sentence a viewer could picture.

- **Only a mood or one image** ("a fractal god that travels through time", "something trippy with jellyfish"): offer three loglines, one line each, each with a different turn. The user picks one. On "surprise me", pick one and say which.
- **Characters and a conflict already given** ("rust giant vs bloom giant"): write one logline. Say in one line what turn it chose, so the user can swap it.
- **Every logline keeps the user's subject.** Three different turns, not three different ideas.

```text
1. A fractal god walks backward through history, and every era it passes gets one shape simpler, until the last cave painting is a single dot.
2. A fractal god tries to visit its own birth, and each step back in time splits it into smaller copies of itself.
3. A clockmaker builds a fractal god to stop time, and it stops everything except the clockmaker.
```

## Beats sized to the length

Pixar's story rules give the spine ("Once upon a time... One day... Until finally") and say to write the ending first. StudioBinder's short-film advice is one character, one situation, one emotional core. For a reel that comes down to three beats around one idea:

| Length | Shots | Beats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 to 15 s | 2 to 3 | Hook, payoff (the turn sits in the cut between them) |
| 15 to 30 s | 3 to 6 | Hook, turn, payoff. A setup shot between hook and turn if there is room |
| 30 to 60 s | 6 to 12 | Hook, setup, one or two turns, payoff |

No platform or story authority gives beats per second. These counts come from shot length: Wan 2.2 makes about 5 s a shot, and LTX-2.5 5 to 10 s, so 20 s is about four shots. A faster cut uses more, shorter shots trimmed from 5 s clips; cuts of 2 to 4 s are common in short-form, but no study backs a number, so that is preference. Hold the payoff longest.

**Default when nothing is given:** a 20 s, 9:16 reel, four shots.

## The hook

- **The first shot is the hook.** It shows the strangest or biggest thing in the piece, or a question the viewer needs answered. Not a slow establishing shot.
- **Aim to land it in the first 1 to 2 seconds.** That number is this skill's target, not a platform rule. TikTok's ad guide asks for the hook within the first 6 seconds and the main message within 3; Meta says "the first few seconds". YouTube's "viewed vs. swiped away" metric is how to check it worked.
- **Hook by picture, not by title card.** Meta names three hook types: a promise of value, a statement of intent, a question. In a story reel, the question is a picture: an iron foot the size of a church, a god made of shapes.
- **The hook shot is often the riskiest** (the biggest scale, the most motion). Make it first ([production.md](./production.md#risky-shots-and-takes)).

## The turn and the payoff

- **The turn** changes what the viewer expects: the fight becomes a hug, the monster is the victim, the god gets smaller.
- **The payoff** shows the result, and holds. Give it the longest shot, often an LTX-2.5 shot of 5 to 10 s, or a Wan shot with a held end.
- **Write the payoff first** (Pixar's rule 7), then the hook that sets it up.
- **One impossible thing** at a time. The rest of the world obeys physics, so the impossible thing reads.
- **A loop** (the last frame matches the first) is a choice, not a rule. If wanted, plan both stills from the same frame of the master.

## The script

Short and visual. Action lines say only what a camera sees. Final Draft keeps action to about five lines a block; for a reel, one or two.

```text
1. EXT. WHEAT VALLEY, DAWN
An iron foot the size of a church slams into the wheat. Rust spreads out through the stalks in a ring.
NARRATOR (V.O., old woman, low and calm): "It came for the last green valley."
```

| Part | Form |
| --- | --- |
| Shot number | Matches the shot list |
| Scene heading | EXT. or INT., the place, the time of day. Only when the place or time changes |
| Action | Present tense, what is seen and heard. No feelings, only what shows them: "her jaw tightens" |
| Speaker | NAME, then in brackets V.O. (narrator, not in the scene), O.S. (in the scene, out of frame), or nothing (on screen), then how they speak |
| Line | In double quotes |

- **Voiceover tells what the picture can't.** Final Draft: voiceover should not repeat what the viewer sees.
- **Say if a mouth is on screen.** A line with no V.O. or O.S. needs lip sync or LTX-2.5's own voice ([production.md](./production.md#gaps)).
- **A narrated piece can use two columns:** picture on the left, sound on the right, row by row. That is the AV script format for ads and narrated video. The shot list already does this, so the script stays in screenplay form.

## How many words fit

ACX puts audiobook narration at about 155 words a minute, and voiceover guides use about 150. That is **2.5 words a second**:

| Length | Words at most | Leave room: aim for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 s | 37 | 25 |
| 30 s | 75 | 50 |
| 60 s | 150 | 100 |

A reel with picture to watch wants far fewer: one short line per beat, and silence for the hook and the payoff if they work alone. ElevenLabs sets speed as a multiplier (0.7 to 1.2), not words a minute, so check real lengths in the voice draft.

## FAIL and PASS

FAIL, for "rust giant vs bloom giant", 20 s:

```text
Two giants meet. They fight for a long time. Then they become friends and the world is saved and everyone is happy. Then the sun sets.
```

No hook, a turn that happens off screen, four endings, and too many beats for four shots.

PASS:

```text
Logline: A giant of moss and branches must stop a rust giant crushing the last green valley, and wins without throwing a punch.
Hook (shot 1): an iron foot slams into the wheat and the wheat rusts.
Turn (shot 3): the bloom giant catches the punch instead of hitting back.
Payoff (shot 4): the rust giant kneels and flowers open across it until it is a hill.
```
